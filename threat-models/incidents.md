# AI security incident register (January 2025 – November 2026)

A dated register of the AI security incidents the threat models in this folder draw on, compiled on
2026-11-24 from primary sources: advisories, CVE and KEV records, threat reports, researcher write-ups,
papers, and court and regulator documents. *Attacks on AI systems* target a model, agent or AI product or
its supply chain; *AI used against other systems or people* is misuse of AI as a tool; *failures and
accidents* are harm with no attacker directing the AI; the last two sections report no real-world
exploitation. The register is not exhaustive, and each threat model cites the entries relevant to it.
Each entry has a stable id (`INC-001` …) that test cases and threat lists can cite as the observed
pattern behind them; ids are never reused.

## Patterns

- **Prompt injection is the leading failure in AI applications and agents, and the damage depends on what
  the agent can do or display.** Exfiltration through rendered images, links and pop-ups (EchoLeak,
  ForcedLeak, CamoLeak, AgentFlayer, the ChatGPT Lockdown Mode bypass), theft of one-time passwords and
  password-manager access, and cross-origin reads by browser agents recur. A common root cause is a gap
  between what the user sees and what the model reads: downscaled images, faint text in screenshots,
  1-pixel or zero-size text, hidden page fields. Most vendor fixes were output and egress controls rather
  than model-level resistance, and two major vendors say prompt injection is not solved.
- **AI platforms are exploited as ordinary web software.** Langflow and LiteLLM account for more than ten
  KEV entries in 2025–2026, and code-execution features (flow validation, MCP test endpoints, custom-code
  guardrails) are the common entry point.
- **Coding agents fail at approval and trust boundaries.** Recurring weaknesses are approval and allowlist
  parsing, configuration that runs before the trust prompt (Claude Code, Codex CLI, Cursor), and agents in
  CI that hold secrets while reading attacker-written text. Both exploited coding-agent events here
  (Amazon Q, Clinejection) went through CI credentials, not the model. Attackers also run victims'
  installed agents as tools (s1ngularity, QUIETVAULT), which Microsoft calls "living off the AI".
- **The AI supply chain is attacked mainly through publishing credentials and marketplaces.** Malicious
  model files and scanner bypasses continue (nullifAI, Picklescan), but the most damaging 2026 events were
  compromised publishing credentials for AI libraries (LiteLLM twice, Microsoft Agent Framework), with
  payloads aimed at AI provider keys. Most MCP CVEs are in client tooling, OAuth handling and tool
  arguments; the confirmed malicious cases are impersonation followed by a malicious update (postmark-mcp)
  and marketplace malware (ClawHub). Hallucinated dependencies now spread through LLM-written skill files.
- **Misuse of general-purpose models moved from assistance to operation.** Early-2025 reports found
  productivity gains and failed public jailbreaks. From mid-2025, commercial coding agents acted as the
  operator on victim networks (GTG-2002, GTG-1002, the Mexican government breach, Anthropic's September
  2026 cases), and safeguards were worked around by splitting tasks into harmless-looking pieces and by
  pretexts (capture-the-flag, student, security firm). Individuals and small groups reached the scale of a
  team, mostly with familiar techniques: weak credentials, known CVEs, exposed services.
- **Malware that calls LLMs is emerging but depends on hosted services.** Run-time LLM use went from
  proofs of concept (PromptLock, PROMPTFLUX, HONESTCUE, PromptSpy) to one state-attributed operational
  family (LAMEHUG, also tracked as PROMPTSTEAL). Most families rely on embedded API keys or hosted
  endpoints, which gives defenders points for detection and takedown. AI services were also used as
  covert command-and-control (SesameOp) and trusted hosting (ClickFix lures on AI share links), and some
  malware carries text aimed at AI analysis tools.
- **Evaluation agents reaching real systems is a new class in 2026.** Anthropic, UK AISI, OpenAI and
  Hugging Face, and WorkOS all report it. Common factors were unintended or deliberately enabled internet
  access, safety classifiers or monitors turned off to measure capability, and impossible or misconfigured
  tasks. Responses included removing live internet access, requiring chain-of-thought and real-time
  monitoring, and third-party review. For restricted-access cyber models, the isolation of evaluation
  environments is part of the attack surface, alongside control of customer access.
- **AI-found vulnerabilities now outpace triage, disclosure and patching.** Counts rose from single CVEs
  (o3 and ksmbd, Big Sleep and SQLite) to hundreds (Opus 4.6) and thousands (Mythos Preview partners).
  Labs now gate their most cyber-capable models (Mythos Preview, GPT-6 Cyber), and Anthropic reports no
  malicious cyber use of Mythos-class models up to August 2026. Microsoft puts median time to
  weaponisation well under 24 hours, and low-quality AI reports ended at least one bug bounty (curl).
- **Guardrails and decision models fail in consistent ways.** Tokenizer gaps (TokenBreak), adversarial
  suffixes (EchoGram, BRANCH), LLM judges that share the weaknesses of the models they guard (OpenAI
  Guardrails), in-band text that redefines the task (counterfeit policy, comments in code under review,
  strings in malware) and attacks on the option label all reverse verdicts, and reversed decisions keep
  their confidence. Every guardrail and scanner tested in these entries had a reported bypass. Defences
  that held in research decide again without the untrusted input, or use deterministic rules where the
  policy can be written down.
- **For voice and image models the harm is fraud and impersonation, and generated media target other
  gates.** Deepfake video calls and voice-clone impersonation of officials drive the voice incidents; no
  exploitation of voice-model APIs was found. Generated media are used against liveness checks, hiring
  interviews and expense audits, and provenance is weak at both ends: watermarks are removable in
  research, and platforms strip provenance metadata. The largest image-generation incident was a
  deployment failure (Grok) that drew regulatory action within weeks; legislators added removal duties
  (US, in force May 2026) and an offence of creating intimate images without consent (UK, in force
  February 2026).
